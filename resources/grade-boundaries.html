Understanding Your Results

Grade Boundaries Explained

Grade boundaries determine what raw mark earns each grade. They change every series and are set by the exam board — not by teachers, schools, or tutors. Here is everything you need to understand them.

One of the most misunderstood aspects of Cambridge and Edexcel examinations is how final grades are determined. Many students revise with a target raw mark in mind — often based on boundaries from a previous year or a social media post — without realising that those boundaries may bear no resemblance to what will be required in the current series. This guide explains how the boundary-setting process actually works, and how to approach your revision intelligently.

What Are Grade Boundaries?

A grade boundary is the minimum raw mark a candidate must achieve to be awarded a specific grade on a specific paper in a specific examination series.

For example: if the A grade boundary for Cambridge A Level Mathematics Paper 1 in the May/June 2025 series is 68 out of 100, then any candidate who scores 68 or above on that paper has met the threshold for an A on that component.

A* A B C D

Each grade (A*, A, B, C, D, E for A Level; A*, A, B, C, D for IGCSE/O Level) has its own boundary — and each boundary is set independently for each paper, each subject, and each exam series.

Why Do Boundaries Change Each Series?

The difficulty level of a paper varies from one series to another — even when the syllabus is identical. A harder paper will have lower grade boundaries; an easier paper will have higher boundaries.

This adjustment process is called grade standardisation. Its purpose is to ensure that a student who sits a particularly difficult paper is not unfairly penalised for the greater challenge — and that a student sitting an easier paper does not benefit from an unjustified advantage.

The boundary-setting process involves senior examiners reviewing statistical distributions, reviewing live scripts, and making judgement calls about where the grade threshold sits relative to the overall quality of the cohort's responses. It is not a simple formula — it is a professional determination made each series.

How Boundary Setting Works in Practice

Step 1: Marking Complete

All candidate scripts are marked by the examiner network. Marks are aggregated and the score distribution across all candidates is analysed by the Awarding Committee.

Step 2: Awarding Committee Review

Senior examiners review "borderline" scripts — papers that fall near predicted grade thresholds — to make qualitative judgements about where the boundary should fall to reflect genuine attainment.

Step 3: Boundaries Published

On results day, final grade boundaries are published by the exam board. Boundaries are published simultaneously with results — there is no advance notice. They cannot be predicted with accuracy beforehand.

Where to Find Official Grade Boundaries

Cambridge Assessment International Education (CAIE)

Official grade threshold documents are published at:

cambridgeinternational.org/exam-administration/results-and-certificates/grade-thresholds/

Documents are typically published on the morning of results day, searchable by subject, series, and paper.

Pearson Edexcel International

Official grade boundaries are published at:

qualifications.pearson.com/en/support/support-topics/results-certification/grade-boundaries.html

Available for IGCSE, IAL, and other Edexcel International qualifications, organised by qualification and series.

Important: Do Not Use Third-Party Boundary Predictions

In the weeks before results day, social media platforms and online forums are filled with posts claiming to "predict" grade boundaries for the upcoming series. These predictions are based on unofficial surveys, anecdotal reports from candidates, and statistical models that do not have access to the full data set used by the Awarding Committee.

Boundaries are published on results day — not before. Never use a third-party boundary prediction as a revision target, and do not make decisions about resits or university applications based on them.

The only accurate, authoritative source of grade boundaries is the exam board's own published documentation. GOALPAc trains students to aim for a performance level comfortably above where they estimate the boundary will fall — maximising across the full mark range rather than targeting a specific raw mark.

How to Use Historical Boundaries Wisely

What Historical Boundaries Can Tell You
  • The approximate range within which boundaries typically fall for a given subject
  • How widely boundaries vary from series to series (indicating paper difficulty volatility)
  • The rough relationship between components — which papers tend to be more boundary-sensitive
What Historical Boundaries Cannot Tell You
  • Exactly where the boundary will fall in the next series
  • Whether a "slightly above last year's A boundary" score will earn an A this year
  • How difficult or easy the current series' paper will be judged to be

GOALPAc's approach: We use historical boundary data to set aspirational performance benchmarks in mock marking, but we always train students to maximise raw marks rather than work backward from a target boundary. A student aiming for 90% is protected against a high-boundary year. A student aiming for the predicted A boundary of 68% is not.

Boundaries are set at the component (paper) level for each individual paper. Where a grade aggregates across multiple components (as in Cambridge A Levels), the board uses a combination of component boundaries and an overall Uniform Mark Scale (UMS) or similar aggregation method to determine the final grade. You should review boundaries for each individual paper you sit.

If you believe there has been a marking error, you can request a clerical check and/or a re-mark through your exam centre. The boundary itself cannot be changed on an individual basis — it applies uniformly to all candidates. However, if a re-mark increases your mark above the boundary, your grade will be revised accordingly.
